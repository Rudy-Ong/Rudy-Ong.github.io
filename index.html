Japanese high vowels have a habit of going quiet. In 好き suki the /u/ sits between two voiceless consonants and loses its voicing; in です desu the final /u/ does the same. Native speakers do it without noticing. Learners often don't — and a recogniser has to account for a vowel that left almost no acoustic trace behind.

Vowel devoicing, detected from audio

Record or upload Japanese speech and the model marks, phone by phone, where devoicing actually happened. Devoiced high vowels are upper-cased in the training transcript (s U k i), so recognition and detection happen in one pass rather than two. The demo brackets them in its output — sh [I] t e.

What devoicing looks like ・ 無声化

Devoicing is invisible. The tongue, jaw and lips do exactly what they would for a voiced vowel — only the vocal folds stop. These two demos show that gap from both sides: a mouth built from six articulatory parameters, and what is left to read when the sound is gone.

Where Japanese high vowels devoice
Context Example Typically
/i/ or /u/ between two voiceless consonants 好き suki, した shita devoiced
High vowel word-final after a voiceless consonant です desu, ます masu devoiced
Vowel carrying the pitch accent varies more likely to keep voicing
Kansai and western dialects — devoice far less than Tokyo speech
